# Essay 11 — The longer timeline
## The in-jokes still don't harden, and Kill James Bond turns out to teach and amend its own instrument

Essay 08 took the measurement this corpus was built for. The worry was that *Kill James Bond!*'s private vocabulary (its SCUM scoring system, the Kronsteen Rosette, the Goodnight Cross, "Waingrow" and the rest) would pile up over the years and harden into a gate: a show that began by joking about an in-group would end up with a real one. Across eight episodes from late 2021 to January 2026 the vocabulary did not get denser, and the essay concluded that the audience is held together by shared position, not by lore.

Eight points is a short series, and the last of them was the highest. Fifteen later full episodes have since been added to the archive, running from Fast Five to Layer Cake, the continuation of the same heist season. That nearly triples the number of episodes and extends the line past the old endpoint. Before counting anything in them I wrote down what I expected, with the conditions that would prove it wrong, and committed it (the registered prediction is in FINDINGS as the extended in-joke test). This essay reports the result. The predictions held. Two things I did not predict matter more, because both contradict claims this book has been making.

### What had to be fixed before counting

The fifteen new transcripts come from a different captioning system than the original eight, and the two systems spell the show's invented words differently: "Cronstein" in one, "Kronsteen" and "Cronsteen" in the other; "Waingrow" against "Waingro". Checking for that turned up a flaw in the original instrument as well. It matched "Goodnight Cross" only as one word, and so had missed five mentions written "Good Night Cross" in the original eight episodes. Essay 08's figures were therefore slight undercounts.

So every number below is reported two ways: with the original word list, untouched, and with a calibrated list that accepts every spelling found in either source. Both were run over all 23 episodes, including a recount of the first eight. The conclusion of essay 08 survives the recount (the calibrated series for the first eight runs from 0.12 to 1.18 mentions per thousand words, against 0.12 to 0.99 before), but the old numbers should be read as the lower bound they were.

A second repair came after the first run. I had planned to find each episode's scoring segment by looking for the word "smarm", the first of SCUM's four axes. The new captions mangle that word into several different non-words, the detector found a scoring segment in under half the episodes, and its output was useless. I replaced it with a marker the captions render reliably: the sentence in which the hosts introduce the scoring system. That repair was made after seeing data, and I flag it as such. The prediction it serves was fixed before.

### The registered result: still flat

**No climb.** Across the 23 episodes in broadcast order, the rank correlation between how late an episode is and how dense its in-joke vocabulary is comes out *negative*: −0.27 with the calibrated list and −0.31 with the original, against a threshold of +0.41 for a reliable upward trend. The fifteen new episodes average 0.27 mentions per thousand words, inside the old range and below the old average of 0.58. Within the new fifteen alone the trend is flat (+0.05).

I am not claiming the vocabulary is *shrinking*. The drop coincides exactly with the change of captioning source, and I cannot separate the two. What can be said is that there is no sign of growth on any version of the measure.

**The count mostly measures the scoring segment.** Sixty per cent of all mentions fall inside the scoring segment, which occupies roughly the last tenth of each episode. Inside it, density runs from about half a mention to eight per thousand words; outside it, most episodes sit near zero. With the scoring segments removed the series is still flat (−0.19). The one exception is the Fast and the Furious episode from essay 08, where talk of "technical" rosettes runs through the whole recap. That episode was the old endpoint, which is part of why the eight-point series looked as if it might be turning upward.

**The vocabulary is, if anything, narrower.** The original eight episodes use about four distinct pieces of apparatus each; the new fifteen use fewer than three. The Kaufman Star and the short-lived GIRTH system appear in none of the fifteen, and the Goodnight Cross in one. What remains is the SCUM system, its "science-based" tag, and the occasional Kronsteen. The same caution about the captioning source applies here.

**The instrument is still explained.** This was the third prediction, and it is where the unpredicted findings begin.

### Kill James Bond re-teaches its instrument every episode

In all fifteen new episodes, at the start of the scoring segment, the hosts recite a fixed formula: the show has a science-based system, it is called the SCUM system, and here is what each letter stands for. Then they score the film on each axis in turn. The formula is word-for-word stable from one episode to the next. It is often delivered as call and response, one voice setting it up and another confirming. In one episode the speaker fluffs the order, asks aloud how many times they have done this, and gets the answer "Hundreds of times." (The transcripts carry no speaker labels, so I don't say which host says what, here or below.)

The recount shows the same recitation in six of the original eight episodes too. It was there all along.

This matters because the book has leaned on a contrast. *The Bechdel Cast* re-explains the Bechdel test from scratch in every episode, and I read that as the mark of a show that imagines a newcomer listening. *Kill James Bond!*, on the brief's description, uses its apparatus without explanation, on the assumption that the listener is an initiate. For the central instrument, that description is wrong. Both shows define their main scoring tool every time they use it. A first-time listener to either show is told what the instrument is.

What survives is narrower. The rest of the apparatus really is used unexplained: nobody says what a Kronsteen is, or a Waingrow. And the two recitations may do different work, a lesson in one case and something closer to a catechism in the other, said by people who know it to people who know it. But I have not hand-compared the two shows' recitations, and the difference in function is a hypothesis, not a finding. The word-count result is simply that the re-teaching is symmetrical.

### Kill James Bond amends its instrument, with reasons

The second finding is one the book had named in advance as something that would count against it. The synthesis argues that the two shows own different kinds of sophistication: *Kill James Bond!* builds new frameworks, while *The Bechdel Cast* questions the validity of its own measuring tool. I wrote that the first show only ever jokes about SCUM and never interrogates it, and I listed "KJB interrogating SCUM" as an observation that would falsify the tidy split.

It happens across four of the new episodes.

In the F9 episode, a host wants to mark the film down for the way people are killed in large numbers with no weight given to it, and says the existing axis, "unprovoked violence", cannot capture that. The reply says what the system is for ("it's meant to elucidate us talking about it") and proposes changing the axis name from "unprovoked" to "unnecessary" when the season reaches The Blues Brothers, because a line in that film supplies the phrase. The proposal is welcomed, along with an alternative someone had already written down: "unquestioned violence". It is not unanimous at first. One voice argues the existing wording already covers a high body count, and another points out that the system was designed for Bond films and was never going to fit everything. They settle it in mock-legal terms, one declaring for originalism and the others for reasoning their way to a new meaning.

Two episodes later, in Blue Collar, the change is announced: "We are changing unprovoked violence to unnecessary violence." Baby Driver recites the new wording, with an aside that the Blues Brothers episode is on its way. In The Blues Brothers itself, when the film reaches the line, the hosts ratify it: "It's unnecessary violence now." Every later episode in the archive recites the amended formula.

Stripped of the bit, this is a construct-validity argument followed by a revision. The hosts identify something the instrument should measure and cannot, state the instrument's purpose, weigh alternatives, change a definition, and keep to the change. They also start doing new analysis with it: in a later episode a host asks whether a film is inviting the audience to question if its violence is *necessary*, a question the old wording did not pose.

There are smaller signs of the same habit. In a later episode a score is adjusted for era: "a three in 1978, which is a four in today's scum system". And the F9 argument itself is conducted as a dispute about how to interpret a founding text. This is the developing case law that essay 01 attributed to *The Bechdel Cast* alone, where it said *Kill James Bond!* just writes new statutes.

### What this does to the book's claims

The claim that the in-jokes do not harden is stronger. It now rests on 23 episodes and three versions of the measure, and the one point that had looked like the start of a climb turns out to be an unusual episode.

The claim that the two shows differ in whom they address needs narrowing. Both re-teach their central instrument every episode. The difference, if there is one, lies in the secondary apparatus and in what the recitation is doing, and the second of those has not been tested.

The claim that only *The Bechdel Cast* reflects on its instrument is falsified as stated. The honest replacement is a difference of frequency and manner. *The Bechdel Cast* questions the Bechdel test routinely and in earnest. *Kill James Bond!* did it once in the available record, over several episodes, packaged as a joke with a film line for a punchline, and then lived by the result. That is consistent with what the book says about each show's stance toward its own seriousness. It is not consistent with "never".

Three limits. The amendment is one event in roughly five years of episodes. All fifteen new transcripts come from one captioning source that differs from the first eight, so nothing here should be read as change over time *in the hosts*. And none of the fifteen is yet part of a matched pair, so nothing new can be said about *The Bechdel Cast* from them.
